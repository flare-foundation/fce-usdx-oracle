⚠ The question

Today, one unavailable backing API blocks the price entirely. Is that the behaviour we want, or should the oracle publish a degraded price computed from the sources that did respond?

The code fails closed: if either Hex endpoint is down, no price is published and the feed ages. That is a deliberate design decision (spec §7.15), but it was made before the implementation revealed that the two backing sources have no redundancy at all — one URL each, no fallback — while the supply reads have three-deep provider redundancy per chain. The asymmetry was not an explicit choice, and it makes the whole feed's availability equal to the availability of Hex's two endpoints.

The question that settles it: is an hour of stale price worse than an hour of price computed from an incomplete formula? Our reading is no — see the analysis below — but the answer commits someone to accepting either staleness risk or misreporting risk, and that is not an engineering call.

Secondary question, same owner: what uptime do we actually get from Hex? This is question 8 on the existing Hex list ("uptime expectations and an escalation contact") and it should be weighted higher than it currently is, because with no fallback source their uptime is our uptime.

Background 1 — why supply is read twice

Asked during review; recording the answer because the reasoning is not obvious from the code alone.

Supply reads and reserve reads are not atomic, and the resulting error has a direction. oracle.Observe brackets the reserve read between two supply reads:

S₁ ← supply        B ← reserves        S₂ ← supply
S  = max(S₁, S₂)
price = min(1, B / S)

The dangerous interleaving is reading reserves first (pre-redemption, high B) and supply after a burn lands (post-redemption, low S). B/S then comes out overstated — and an overstated price hides a shortfall, which is the one failure this oracle exists to prevent.

Taking max(S₁, S₂) means the larger denominator, so any timing skew pushes the price down. The error is always in the direction that fails safe.

Not theoretical at this scale: the 1,000,000 USDX Flare burn on 31 Jul 2026 was 4.8% of supply in a single event, far larger than any deviation threshold we would set. And redemptions cluster precisely during the stress when the price matters most. The cost is one extra RPC round per observation.

Caveat: the double-read covers less than the framing suggests

oracle.checkWindow computes the consistency spread over Reading.RetrievedAt — when we received the response. But Reading.AsOf is Hex's own last_update, and its doc comment states that "the gap between this and the supply reads is the TOCTOU exposure in spec §6.1."

That gap is not in the window check. It is bounded only by MaxAge, which for a source refreshing on a ~10 minute tick has to be at least 10 minutes. So the double-read tightens a 30-second bracket sitting inside a staleness gap roughly twenty times larger — and it cannot help there: if B is ten minutes stale and a burn landed five minutes ago, both S₁ and S₂ are post-burn while B is pre-burn. That is the bad direction, and max() cannot see it.

The double-read is still correct and worth keeping. It simply protects a smaller exposure than the design language implies.

OPEN: fold AsOf into checkWindow, or state explicitly that MaxAge is the real TOCTOU bound and the consistency window only covers read skew. Right now the two documents disagree with the code.

Background 2 — what actually happens when a source is down

Tolerance is layered, and it is asymmetric. This is the state of the implementation as of 3 Aug 2026.

LayerBehaviourVerdict
RPC providers
(within a chain)		 agreedBlock skips providers that error; fails only when surviving providers < Quorum. readTotalSupply then requires Quorum identical answers. Tolerant. 3 providers at quorum 2 → one dead provider costs nothing.
Chains
(Flare / Ethereum / Songbird)		 readAllChains fails the whole observation if any chain fails. Intolerant — and correctly so. Dropping Songbird's 1.16M would manufacture a 4.5% depeg.
Backing APIs
(bonds / cash)		 readBacking aborts on either error. Two attempts, 250 ms apart, transient faults only — 5xx and 429 retry, 4xx and malformed bodies do not. Intolerant, and with no redundancy — a single URL per source.

Identical results are required rather than a median because a read at a fixed block is deterministic: two honest providers cannot disagree, so disagreement means one is wrong or lying and there is no safe way to average that.

The gap is the third row. Supply has three-deep redundancy; backing has none.

What "fail closed" actually does

Worth stating precisely, because the phrase sounds worse than the behaviour:

  • No new price is published.
  • UsdxPriceStore keeps the last accepted price and its observedAt.
  • UsdxCustomFeed.getCurrentFeed() never reverts — it returns the last price with an ageing timestamp. Even a pause freezes rather than throws, specifically because FtsoV2 forwards the call and a revert would become a simultaneous liveness outage for every consumer at once.

The failure mode is stale and visibly so, not broken. Consumers are required to check the timestamp, and that requirement is already on the consumer-docs TODO.

The analysis: should a cash outage block the price?

Cash reserves are ~205,304 against ~25,452,544 of bonds — 0.8% of backing. Omitting cash understates backing, which is the conservative direction. The tempting conclusion is that dropping it is harmless.

Two reasons we recommend against it.

1. Structural — a degraded price is indistinguishable from a full one

report.PriceReport is {ChainID, Store, FeedID, Price, Decimals, ObservedAt, InstructionID}. There is no field for "computed without cash", and the on-chain payload is price-only by decision (spec §4.1, Hex confidentiality). GET /state cannot carry it either, for the same reason.

So a degraded price would reach consumers and auditors as an ordinary one. We would be silently changing what the published number means, with no channel to say so. The signature's entire purpose is that the published price is the one the attested code computed to the published definition.

2. Numeric — and this is the decisive one

Under par operations R_bonds ≈ S (observed 31 Jul), so dropping cash changes nothing: both paths give exactly 1.0 after the par cap. It looks free.

But R_bonds is a genuine asset measure that can fall below supply (§3.0, resolved: it is a bank-reported balance) — and when it does, cash is precisely the buffer that decides whether the price is below par:

ScenarioWith cashWithout cash
Bonds = 1.00 · S1.000 (capped)1.000
Bonds = 0.99 · S0.9980.990

On a depeg feed with alerting attached, 0.8 percentage points is the signal. Degradation would manufacture depegs exactly when the feed matters most.

Therefore

With §3.0 resolved — R_bonds is a real balance that can fall below supply — the numeric argument applies today. Dropping cash would suppress exactly the buffer that decides whether a depeg prints, which is a strong reason not to degrade.

Recommendation

The fix for the availability concern is redundancy, not degradation.

  1. Give the backing sources fallbacks, the way supply already has them. One URL per source is the single point of failure for the entire feed. For the public bond figure a second source may be feasible. For the private cash endpoint it is Hex's one endpoint, so it becomes an uptime/SLA conversation — which is why the secondary question above matters more than its current position on the list suggests.
  2. Resolve the AsOf / RetrievedAt inconsistency in checkWindow, or document that MaxAge is the real bound.
  3. Do not implement graceful degradation. §3.0 is resolved: R_bonds is a real asset measure, so degradation is actively harmful — it would drop the cash buffer that decides whether a depeg prints.

Neither (1) nor (2) is a correctness bug in the current code. They are gaps between what the design claims and what the implementation guarantees.

Deferred — source-unreachable alerting, in the keeper

Status: to think about later. Not designed, not scheduled. Recorded here so the placement decision is not relitigated.

Fail-closed is only a safe default if somebody finds out. Today an unreachable source produces a failed observation and a sourceHealth entry, and nothing pages anyone. The Price < 1 alert is already on the plan as a business event; source-unreachable is the missing operational twin — and it is the one that fires first, because a source outage precedes the staleness a consumer would eventually notice.

This belongs in the keeper, not the extension

The extension is the wrong place, for reasons that are structural rather than stylistic:

  • The host allowlist is baked into the image. allowedHosts in the embedded config is code-hash-pinned precisely so configured endpoints can only ever be attested hosts. Adding a webhook or paging endpoint would mean a new code hash — re-attestation and a version registration — for a purely operational concern.
  • Alerting credentials would become enclave secrets. A PagerDuty key or Slack webhook would join the secret inventory, the provisioning path, and the disclosure surface, for no security benefit.
  • ActionResult.Log is public, so the extension is deliberately constrained in what it can say. The keeper is not, and it never sees reserve figures anyway — the payload is price-only.
  • The extension cannot report its own absence. If the TEE is down, no ActionResult comes back at all. Only the caller can detect that, and the caller is the keeper.

The keeper already schedules updates, polls the proxy for results, and submits to the store. It is the only component that sees the whole round trip.

Signals already available to it

No extension change is needed; the data is already on the wire.

SourceSignal
ActionResult.Log A failure.Failure from the closed vocabulary — the error class is the alert key
Proxy GET /info → TEEState.state sourceHealth map, per source: ok / degraded / down / unknown
Same failureCount, lastErrorClass, updateCount, lastObservedAt, readiness
Keeper's own timeout No result at all → TEE or proxy unreachable, distinct from a source outage

The classes that matter for this alert:

ClassSourceDown     BackingUnavailable, BackingTimeout, SupplyUnavailable, SupplyTimeout
ClassQuorumFailed   SupplyNoQuorum
ClassSourceInvalid  BackingMalformed, BackingStale, BackingCurrency
ClassNotConfigured  NotConfigured        (secrets lost to a restart — memory-only by design)

ClassSourceDown and ClassQuorumFailed are the reachability alerts. ClassSourceInvalid is a different kind of problem — the source answered but said something we refuse to use — and should page differently, because a shape change from Hex needs a human reading the response, not a retry.

Design points to settle when this is picked up

  • Alert on sustained failure, not single events. FCC is fire-and-forget with no delivery guarantee, and UsdxPriceStore treats a rejected submission as normal operation. A single failed observation is noise. N consecutive failures, or a time threshold, is signal.
  • Distinguish "a machine" from "the sources". getRandomTeeIds picks one machine per instruction, so one failure may be one bad machine. Alerting on a single result cannot tell the difference; correlating across machines can.
  • Deduplicate across keeper instances. The plan calls for ≥2 idempotent keepers. Both will observe the same outage and must not both page.
  • sourceHealth is a snapshot, not a probe. It is written only when an observation runs. If the keeper stops requesting, the last values persist and continue to read ok — the failure mode finding 8 was written about, one level up. Freshness of the health map needs its own check.
  • Staleness is the consumer-facing alert and is separate. Source-down is a cause; feed age past 2× the update interval is the effect consumers actually feel. Both are wanted; only the second is a true page.
  • Per-source or per-chain granularity is still open (finding 8): rpc_flare: "ok" hides one-of-three providers being down, which is exactly the state worth seeing before the second one goes and quorum fails.

Related

This is the mitigation half of the open question at the top of this document. If the answer there is "keep failing closed", then alerting is what makes that acceptable, and it should be scheduled alongside — not after — the decision.
